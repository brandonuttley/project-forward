# Escalation Memo

When a blocker is outside your authority to resolve, escalation is the move. Done badly, escalation looks like complaining or covering yourself. Done well, it surfaces a specific problem to the specific person who can solve it, with the specific decision you need them to make. The escalation memo forces that clarity.

**When to use:** A blocker you've genuinely tried to resolve at your level and can't. A risk that needs executive intervention. A cross-functional dispute the involved parties can't settle.

**When NOT to use:** Frustration. Routine status. Things you haven't actually tried to resolve yourself first. Escalation that bypasses your direct chain without good reason.

**Format default:** Email-formatted markdown for most cases. docx if it's going to multiple executives or needs to land in a formal record.

---

# Escalation: [Specific Issue]

**To:** [Name, role — the person with authority to unblock]
**Cc:** [Direct manager, sponsor, or others who need awareness]
**From:** [Project Lead]
**Date:** [Date]
**Re:** [One-sentence summary]
**Response needed by:** [Specific date and time]

## What's blocked

*One paragraph. Specific. What can't move forward, and what becomes impossible if it stays blocked.*

[e.g., "The vendor contract for the CRM integration has been in Legal review for three weeks against a five-business-day SLA. Until it's signed, we can't begin technical integration work. Every additional week of delay pushes our Oct 1 launch by an equivalent amount."]

## What I've tried

*Show that you've earned the right to escalate. Specifics, not generalities.*

- [e.g., "Followed up with Legal contact on 8/3, 8/10, and 8/15 — was told 'this week' each time"]
- [e.g., "Engaged Legal manager on 8/17 to understand the bottleneck — no clear path forward"]
- [e.g., "Confirmed the contract terms haven't changed and there's no substantive blocker, only capacity"]

## What I need from you

*The specific decision or intervention. Be exact.*

[e.g., "I need you to either (a) push Legal leadership to prioritize this contract by 8/22, or (b) authorize me to engage outside counsel to handle the review, with the associated cost."]

## Why this is the right escalation point

*Address the implicit "why are you bothering me with this" question.*

[e.g., "This is in your area because Legal's prioritization isn't something the project team can influence directly, and the cost of outside counsel exceeds my approval threshold."]

## Cost of inaction

*What happens if nothing is done. Quantified where possible.*

- **Schedule:** [Specific impact on dates]
- **Budget:** [Cost of delay, if calculable]
- **Reputational / strategic:** [What matters beyond money and dates]
- **Downstream:** [What else gets affected]

## What I'm not asking you to do

*Optional but powerful. Reduces the risk of overcorrection.*

[e.g., "Not asking you to override Legal's process or escalate above the Legal lead. Just asking for prioritization within the existing process, or authorization for the alternative path."]

---

## Notes for the PM

- **Lead with what's blocked, not with the history.** Executives don't have time for narrative. The first paragraph should answer "what do you need from me?"
- **Earn the escalation.** If "what I've tried" is thin, the executive will (correctly) bounce it back.
- **Be precise about the ask.** "Help me unblock this" is too vague. "Approve this expense / override this process / make this decision" is escalatable.
- **Time-bound the response.** Without a date, escalations sit in inboxes. With a date, the executive knows when it becomes their problem.
- **Don't escalate twice in 48 hours.** If you escalated on Monday, give the recipient until at least the deadline you set before re-escalating up the chain.
- **Cc with intent.** Cc'ing the sponsor or your manager is fine if they need awareness. Cc'ing the entire executive team is performative.

## When the escalation isn't working

If the executive doesn't respond, or responds non-substantively:

1. **First follow-up (after the deadline):** "Following up on the escalation below. The cost of inaction continues to compound. Need a decision by [new date] or we'll need to take the alternative path of [specific action]."
2. **Second follow-up (after second deadline):** Notify your direct manager (if not already) and request guidance on whether to escalate further up the chain. Document what's happened.
3. **Third tier:** Genuine impasse. Time to involve your sponsor and have a candid conversation about whether this project has the executive support to succeed.

A project that can't get executive responsiveness after three attempts has a sponsorship problem, not an escalation problem. That's a finding worth surfacing.
