# First 30 Days: Taking Over a Stalled or Inherited Program

The structured plan for your first month on a project you didn't start. Whether you've inherited a stalled initiative, replaced a departing PM, or been parachuted into a mess — the first 30 days set the trajectory. Most people skip the diagnostic phase and start "fixing." That's the mistake.

**When to use:** You're new to a project that already exists. Especially valuable when the project is in trouble, the prior PM left, or stakeholders are skeptical.

**When NOT to use:** A project you started yourself. Use the kickoff agenda and project charter instead.

**Format default:** Markdown for personal use. xlsx if you want to track completion.

---

# First 30 Days Plan: [Initiative Name]

**PM:** [Your name]
**Start date:** [Date]
**30-day mark:** [Date]
**Audience:** Yourself, plus your manager and sponsor for visibility

## The principle

The first 30 days are diagnostic, not corrective. Your job is to understand what's actually happening before you change anything material. People will pressure you to act fast. Resist. The hardest part of a turnaround is the first month of holding back.

The output of 30 days: a clear-eyed assessment of where the project is, what's broken, and a 60-day plan with specific moves. Not a hero rescue.

## Week 1: Listen and read

**Goal:** Build a baseline understanding without making any commitments.

### Read

- [ ] Original project charter (if it exists)
- [ ] Last 4 weeks of status reports
- [ ] Latest RAID log
- [ ] Decision log
- [ ] Recent steering committee materials
- [ ] All major contracts and SOWs
- [ ] Sponsor's last public communications about the project
- [ ] Any post-mortem or lessons-learned docs from related work

### Meet (45-60 min, listen-only)

- [ ] **Sponsor** — Their goals, concerns, what success looks like to them, what they'd change about how it's been running
- [ ] **Direct manager** (if not the sponsor) — Their perspective, what they expect from you, how you'll work together
- [ ] **Each workstream owner** — What's working, what's not, what they need
- [ ] **Each consulted party** (Legal, Compliance, Security) — Their view of the project and any concerns
- [ ] **Two or three contributors** — Ground-truth about how the work is actually getting done
- [ ] **Departing PM** if accessible — Their honest read on where things stand and what they'd do differently

### Listening discipline

- Same questions to everyone, plus situational follow-ups
- Capture verbatim quotes when something is striking
- Don't promise anything in week one. "I'm in listening mode for the first two weeks" is a perfectly fine answer to most asks.
- Watch for the gaps — where stakeholders' stories diverge is often where the real problem lives

### Standard listening questions

1. What does success look like for this project from your perspective?
2. What's the single biggest risk you see right now?
3. What's working well that we should keep?
4. What would you change if you could?
5. What do you need from me that you haven't been getting?
6. Who else should I talk to?

## Week 2: Synthesize and verify

**Goal:** Form an honest assessment. Pressure-test it before acting on it.

### Activities

- [ ] Map the actual work happening (vs. what the plan says)
- [ ] Identify the top 3 risks based on what you've heard, not the prior RAID log
- [ ] Identify the top 3 things working well that you don't want to disrupt
- [ ] Test your assessment against 2-3 trusted contributors — does this match what they see?
- [ ] Update (or rebuild) the stakeholder map based on what you've learned about who actually decides

### Common findings in week 2

- The official charter doesn't reflect what people are actually building
- A "consulted" function (often Legal or Security) was never properly engaged and is about to surface a major concern
- One workstream is actually 6 weeks behind what the status reports show
- The sponsor and a key stakeholder have fundamentally different views of scope
- The team has lost confidence and is going through motions

If you find one of these, that's your top priority for weeks 3-4.

## Week 3: Re-anchor

**Goal:** Make the smallest set of changes that put the project on solid ground.

### Re-anchor moves (pick the ones that apply)

- [ ] **Updated charter or charter addendum** — If the official charter is wrong or stale, fix it. Get sponsor sign-off on the corrected version.
- [ ] **Restored cadence** — Reinstate (or fix) weekly status, working sessions, steering review
- [ ] **Honest status reset** — One status report that says where things actually are. Yellow or red where appropriate. Don't sugarcoat.
- [ ] **Refreshed RAID log** — Reflect what you've learned. Close stale entries, add what's been missed.
- [ ] **Stakeholder re-engagement** — Reach out to anyone who's drifted, especially Legal/Compliance/Security. Get them re-committed with specific dates.
- [ ] **Clarify decision rights** — If there's ambiguity about who decides what, name it and resolve it.

### What NOT to do in week 3

- Don't replace the team
- Don't propose massive scope changes
- Don't run a public "fresh start" meeting
- Don't undermine the prior PM in conversation
- Don't commit to dates you don't know you can hit

## Week 4: 60-day plan

**Goal:** A specific, sponsor-approved plan for the next 60 days, grounded in what you've learned.

### Deliverables by end of week 4

- [ ] **30-day findings memo** — One page. What you found. What's working. What's broken. What you've already done.
- [ ] **60-day plan** — Specific moves with owners and dates. Not a vague "stabilize the project" — concrete deliverables.
- [ ] **Updated charter** (if not done in week 3)
- [ ] **Updated RAID log** with current top 5 risks
- [ ] **Sponsor 1:1** to walk through the findings and the 60-day plan, get sign-off

### Findings memo structure

1. **Where the project stands today** (one paragraph)
2. **What's working that we'll preserve** (3 bullets)
3. **What's broken and how we'll fix it** (3-5 items, each with a specific intervention)
4. **What's at risk in the next 60 days** (top 3)
5. **What I need from you** (sponsor commitments, escalations, unblocks)

## Notes for the PM

- The hardest part is resisting the urge to be helpful in week 1. Stakeholders will give you problems to solve. Take notes. Do not solve them yet.
- Do not throw the prior PM under the bus, even if they earned it. Stakeholders are watching how you talk about absent people. They're inferring how you'll talk about them.
- The 30-day mark is your single best moment to reset expectations. After that, every difficult truth gets harder to surface. Be honest in the findings memo.
- If you genuinely can't see a path to success after 30 days, that's a finding too. Surface it cleanly to your sponsor with specific reasoning. A PM who can name an unwinnable situation is more valuable than one who pretends.

## Challenge questions for the 30-day mark

Before you write the findings memo, sit with these. The honest answers shape what kind of plan the next 60 days needs.

- **Is the project actually solving a real problem, or executing on a solution someone got attached to?** Inherited projects often have momentum that outlasts their rationale. If the original "why" is now weak, the next 60 days might need to be about scope reduction or a graceful wind-down, not acceleration.
- **Does the sponsor still want this?** Sponsors lose interest. Their priorities shift. Confirm directly that this project is still on their top-five list. If it's not, surface that — running a project the sponsor has quietly abandoned is one of the most demoralizing situations in PM work, and the only fix is making the abandonment explicit.
- **Are the people on the project the right people, and do they want to be here?** Inherited teams sometimes include people who joined under different leadership and have checked out. You don't have to replace anyone in the first 30 days — but you should know who's all-in and who's drifting, because the 60-day plan depends on it.
- **What did the prior PM see that you haven't seen yet?** Even if they left under bad terms, they had context you don't. If you can talk to them, do. If you can't, the gaps in the documentation are themselves data — write down what you wish was documented and look for it.
- **What's the honest probability this project succeeds as currently scoped?** If your gut says under 50%, the 60-day plan is not "stabilize" — it's either "rescope" or "make the case to leadership for a different direction." Be willing to name this.
