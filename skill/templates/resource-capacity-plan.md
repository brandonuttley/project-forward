# Resource / Capacity Planning Worksheet

A structured view of what your team has committed to versus what they can actually deliver. The artifact that prevents the most common marketing/program failure mode: silently overcommitting and discovering it in week six. Especially valuable for marketing program management where multiple campaigns share resources.

**When to use:** Quarterly planning, when new initiatives land, when team composition changes, or when "we feel overcommitted" needs to become "here's the math."

**When NOT to use:** Single-project, single-team work. Capacity planning is overhead when the team only has one initiative.

**Format default:** xlsx — this is fundamentally tabular and benefits from formulas. Markdown works for a one-time analysis or a small team.

**Period covered:** [Q3 2025 / Aug-Oct / etc.]
**Last updated:** [Date]
**Owner:** [Program lead or marketing PM]

## The principle

Capacity planning is honest math. The goal isn't to maximize utilization — it's to surface the gap between what's been promised and what's possible, before that gap causes a project to fail or a team to burn out.

A good capacity plan answers two questions:
1. Can we actually do everything we've committed to?
2. If not, what's the prioritization conversation we need to have with leadership?

## Step 3: The reality check

| Comparison | Hours |
|---|---|
| Total team weekly capacity (from Step 1) | |
| Total weekly demand from active and committed work (from Step 2) | |
| **Gap** (negative = overcommitted) | |
| **Utilization rate** (demand ÷ capacity) | |

**Reading the numbers:**
- **Under 75% utilization:** Healthy. Room for unexpected work, deeper thinking, or new initiatives.
- **75-95% utilization:** Working. Realistic for sustained periods. Watch for individual hot spots.
- **95-110% utilization:** Stretched. Sustainable for short periods. Quality and morale will degrade if extended.
- **Over 110%:** Overcommitted. Something will fail or someone will burn out. The math doesn't work.

**Common patterns:**
- Senior individuals over-committed because they're the only ones with certain expertise
- Generalists pulled into multiple workstreams without anyone tracking the total
- One person carrying three "high-priority" initiatives because no one's prioritized

## Step 5: What we'd need to do all of it

If the math says you're overcommitted, what would close the gap? Document the options, even if they aren't viable, so the conversation with leadership has substance.

| Option | What it costs | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Add [N] FTEs / contractors | $X | Closes the gap; takes 6-8 weeks to onboard |
| Defer [Initiative X] to next quarter | Stakeholder disappointment | Frees [N] hours/week immediately |
| Cut scope on [Initiative Y] | Reduced impact of Y | Frees [N] hours/week starting [date] |
| Accept 15% utilization overage | Quality and morale risk | Closes the gap on paper |
| **No-action option:** what fails | [Specific projects that miss dates or quality bars] | (this is what happens by default if leadership doesn't decide) |

## Step 6: The recommendation to leadership

*Capacity planning is only useful if it drives a decision. Surface the recommendation clearly.*

## Notes for the PM / program lead

- **The math is the point.** Capacity discussions devolve into vibes ("the team is stretched") without numbers. Numbers force specificity.
- **Don't pretend.** A capacity plan that shows healthy utilization while everyone on the team is working weekends is a document that lies. Recalculate the inputs.
- **Run it quarterly minimum.** Capacity drifts. New initiatives land without anyone subtracting old ones.
- **Share with leadership.** A capacity plan that lives only in your head doesn't drive decisions. Surface it in a steering committee or sponsor 1:1.
- **For marketing program management specifically:** This is one of the most underused tools in marketing operations. Most marketing teams discover they're overcommitted in week six of a quarter. Capacity planning surfaces it in week one.

## Common failure modes

- **Optimistic individual estimates.** People estimate based on what they hope to deliver, not what they typically deliver. Use historical actuals where possible.
- **Missing "the other work."** Tickets, ad-hoc requests, urgent fires from leadership. Reserve 10-15% of capacity for these or pretend they don't exist (which fails).
- **No version control.** Capacity plans get stale fast. Date them and revisit them. A six-month-old plan is decoration.
- **Aggregating away the problem.** "On average we're at 90%" can hide that two people are at 150%. Always check individual hot spots.
